Climate Risks · IDSI 51003 · sustainability consultant brief Lignite exit: 31 Mar 2030

Is RWE on track for its 2030 coal exit?

The lignite exit on 31 March 2030 is fixed by law. We test whether RWE's transition is credible as a business: does its new wind and solar replace the output of the coal it closes, and has RWE ever built at the pace this requires?

Our answer

Not on the projects visible today. The pace needed is close to RWE's best record and within its 2026 guidance, but named, dated projects replace only about half of the coal output.

H0 is evaluated through two criteria set after our project definition, before the final analysis: C1 (coverage likely, P ≥ 0.66) is not met on identifiable projects; C2 (pace within RWE's best 3-year record, coverage-adjusted basis) narrowly fails. RWE's coal timeline is aligned with the IEA Net Zero milestone; the replacement is not, on identifiable projects.

1 · The sector

Europe's coal sits where the pipeline isn't

Europe (EU-27, UK, Norway, Switzerland) still runs 77.5 GW of coal, 70% of it in Germany and Poland. At €100 per tonne of CO₂ that fleet's carbon bill is about €35 bn a year, and two RWE plants are in Europe's top five. The countries and companies with the most coal have the smallest dated wind and solar pipelines.

2 · The data audit

Four corrections before any result

Eemshaven belongs to RWE. GEM lists this Dutch hard-coal plant (2 × 800 MW in GEM; RWE quotes 1,560–1,580 MW) under Essent / E.ON, a leftover of the 2019 asset swap. We add it back, counting 75% of its output as coal.

The pipeline is mostly undated. Only about a third of RWE's wind and solar pipeline capacity has a start year in GEM. Undated projects are left out of the main case.

GEM statuses lag reality. Nordseecluster A and B, Thor and OranjeWind are listed as "pre-construction" but are under construction; F.E.W. Baltic II was sold to PGE; Dogger Bank South and Awel y Môr have public commissioning dates after 2030. We reclassified every RWE offshore project from public sources.

Coverage. GEM sees about 64% of the wind and solar RWE reports. Every result is shown on a GEM-visible and a coverage-adjusted basis, and we never compare numbers across bases.

Utilisation. Fitting our model on RWE's official Scope 1 emissions (five years, 2019–2025) gives a factor B = 0.92 (90% CI 0.83–1.02), drawn in the Monte Carlo: the coal output to replace uses an effective load factor of 0.46.

5 · The credibility test

The pace is close to RWE's record. The projects are not named yet.

Replacing the coal output needs about 11.1 GW of new wind and solar, so 2.23 GW per year to the end of 2030. On the decisive coverage-adjusted basis, that is 1.14 times RWE's best three years (1.8 times as seen in GEM; 0.93 times RWE's reported 2024–25 commissioning, which includes batteries). The pace implied by the identifiable pipeline, 1.15 GW per year, is within RWE's history: the shortfall comes from projects that are not yet named and dated, while RWE's March 2026 guidance would be enough.

6 · Going further

Emissions, carbon cost and firm capacity

RWE's SBTi-validated target is −71.1% Scope 1+2 emissions per MWh by 2030 versus 2022. RWE publishes 0.55 tCO₂e/MWh for 2022 (0.43 in 2025), so the 2030 target is 0.159. Our model reproduces the 2025 value (0.44). Under our model assumptions, the 2030 intensity stays above the target in every scenario, mainly because of the gas fleet; with a five-point calibration and constant gas utilisation, read this as a warning rather than a prediction.

7 · What we advise

Judge RWE on named, dated projects

  1. Investors: judge RWE on named, dated projects: track the final investment decision on Norfolk Vanguard, progress on Nordseecluster B, commissioned GW per year against about 2.2 GW, and the share of the 2031 guidance attached to identified projects.
  2. RWE: a dated 2026–2030 project list would close most of the credibility gap we measure. The SBTi intensity target also needs a plan for gas utilisation.
  3. Policymakers: the coal exit is legally fixed, but German firm capacity after 2030 is a system question. Timely flexible-capacity tenders are the lever; a lignite reserve adds emissions and carbon exposure.

Limits

GEM ownership coverage (64%) and a mostly undated pipeline make our main case a lower bound by construction. Sector build rates (resolved projects only, global), the 0.8 slippage factor, the offshore probabilities and the correlation are assumptions; our back-test suggests the 90% intervals are still too narrow. Capacity factors are constant; utilisation is calibrated on five years. Norfolk Vanguard's investment decision was targeted for summer 2026; we found no public confirmation as of 8 November 2026.
